Arizona trail etiquette · A lesson worth teaching

Don’t Be That Hiker

The trail is not your trash can, dog park, concert venue, shortcut, or canvas. Enjoy it fully—and leave it ready for the next family.

By Mark Stephenson, RNArizona father, hiker, and cardiac catheterization laboratory nurse since 2003Published September 5, 2026

The short version

Take pictures. Take your trash. Keep control of your people and pets. Give others room. Leave rocks, plants, wildlife, artifacts, and trail markers exactly as you found them. Before visiting, check the rules for that specific park or forest because leash rules, trail access, waste disposal, and permitted uses vary.

In plain English: don’t be an asshole on the trail

You do not need a complicated outdoor code. If your choice damages the land, risks another person, scares wildlife, forces everyone to hear you, or leaves somebody else to clean up after you, do something different.

The desert is not a blank wall

Painting, scratching, carving, or writing on a rock is vandalism—not a memory. The National Park Service explains that graffiti removal can require brushes, chemicals, grinders, or even sledgehammers, and the rock may remain discolored afterward. It is especially serious around petroglyphs, archaeological sites, and historic structures.

If you find graffiti, do not try to clean it yourself. Solvents, scrubbing, painting over it, or chipping the surface can make the damage worse. Take a photo without touching the site, save the location, and report it to the responsible park, forest, city, or land manager.

Eight ways not to be that hiker

1. Pack out every bit of trashThat includes fruit peels, sunflower-seed shells, tissues, toilet paper, micro-trash, and tied dog-waste bags. “I’ll grab it later” is how it stays there.
2. Keep your music to yourselfUse earbuds at a safe volume. Other people came for wind, birds, quiet, and conversation—not your portable speaker.
3. Stay on the established trailDo not cut switchbacks or widen the route to avoid mud. Desert vegetation and living soil crust can be damaged by a single careless step and recover slowly.
4. Control your dogFollow local leash rules, keep the dog close when passing, prevent approaches to people or animals, and carry out the poop bag. “Friendly” is not the same as under control.
5. Yield without creating new damageFollow signs. On many shared trails, wheels yield to heels, and hikers yield to horses. Step to a durable spot when safe, speak calmly around horses, and communicate before passing.
6. Let uphill hikers keep their rhythmWhen two hiking groups meet on a narrow grade, give the climbing group space unless they choose to stop. Be human about it: say hello and work it out safely.
7. Leave wildlife wildGive animals room, secure food, and never feed or chase them for a picture. Back up when your presence changes an animal’s behavior.
8. Leave rocks and history aloneDo not take artifacts, carve names, stack decorative cairns, or alter official route markers. A photo is the souvenir that leaves the place intact.

The family version: leave it better without rearranging it

Kids copy the trail behavior they see. Give them three jobs: spot tiny trash, greet people you pass, and protect every living thing by looking without touching. Carry a small trash bag, but do not ask children to pick up sharp, contaminated, or unknown objects.

Our family rule: nobody should be able to tell we were here—except that we may have carried out a safe piece of litter.

Parking lots, viewpoints, and photo stops count too

  • Park only in legal spaces; do not crush roadside vegetation or block emergency access.
  • Make room after taking your photo instead of occupying the viewpoint for a full production.
  • Keep the group together without blocking the trail.
  • Let faster hikers pass when there is a safe, durable place to do it.
  • Obey closures, barriers, permits, and posted use restrictions—even when social media shows somebody ignoring them.

What to do when somebody else behaves badly

Protect your family first. Do not turn trail etiquette into a confrontation. If the behavior is dangerous, destructive, or illegal, document it only from a safe distance and report the time, location, description, and any photo to the land manager or ranger. Call 911 for an immediate threat to life or safety.
